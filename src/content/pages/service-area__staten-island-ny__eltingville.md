## Roofing in Eltingville: what we see on these homes

Eltingville is a South Shore neighborhood of mostly detached one- and two-family homes between Great Kills and Annadale. Much of it was built in the decades after the Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge opened in 1964, so many roofs here have already been replaced once or twice, and some still have every old layer on them.

### Split-levels and high ranches

These homes have several roof planes at different heights, often with a low-slope section over the garage. Low sections that were shingled anyway are a common leak source, because shingles need a slope of at least 2 inches per foot.

### Capes and colonials

Dormers are where capes leak most: the step flashing where the dormer walls meet the roof wears out long before the shingles do.

### Roofs with two or three layers

Every extra layer adds weight, traps heat and hides rot in the deck. We tear everything off, replace damaged decking and start the new roof on a clean, solid surface.

## Why attic ventilation matters on Eltingville roofs

A roof wears out from below as much as from above. When an attic can't breathe, heat in summer bakes the shingles, and warm, moist air in winter condenses on the underside of the deck and melts snow into ice dams. On older Eltingville homes we often find soffit vents painted or insulated shut and no ridge vent at all.

- **Intake at the eaves.** Soffit vents let cool air into the attic.
- **Exhaust at the ridge.** A ridge vent lets hot, moist air out along the whole peak.
- **Ice-and-water shield at the edges.** A self-sealing membrane along the eaves and in the valleys stops ice-dam water from getting in.
- **Insulation that stays in place.** Baffles keep insulation from blocking the soffit vents.

Balanced ventilation helps the new shingles reach their full lifespan and is part of what keeps a shingle warranty valid. Read more about [attic ventilation](/residential-roofing/attic-ventilation/).
