## What roof sheathing does

- **Gives the roof a solid, flat surface** for underlayment and shingles or membrane
- **Holds the nails and fasteners** that keep your roof on in high wind
- **Stiffens the roof structure**, tying rafters together
- **Spreads loads** like snow and foot traffic

## Common types of roof sheathing

### Plywood

Thin wood layers glued in alternating directions. Strong, holds nails well and dries out better if it gets wet.

### OSB (oriented strand board)

Wood strands pressed with resin. Uniform, strong and less expensive. The most common decking on newer homes.

### Plank sheathing

Solid boards with gaps, found on many older NYC houses and row homes. Often needs to be overlaid with plywood for modern roofing.

## Sheathing thickness

Roof sheathing is commonly 7/16" to 5/8" thick, depending on rafter spacing, roof load and local code. Thicker decking is stiffer and holds fasteners better. Your roofer should match or upgrade what's there and follow manufacturer requirements.

## Signs your roof sheathing is damaged

- **Sagging** between rafters or a wavy roof line
- **A soft, spongy feel** when walking the roof
- **Water stains, dark spots or mold** on the underside, visible from the attic
- **Daylight** through the boards
- **Nails backing out** or shingles that won't stay put

## Why sheathing matters when you replace your roof

A new roof is only as good as the deck under it. Installing over rotted or delaminated sheathing leads to sagging, nail pops and leaks, and manufacturers can deny warranty claims when their products are installed over an unsound deck.

That's why a proper [roof replacement](/residential-roofing/roof-replacement/) includes a full tear-off and a board-by-board inspection of the deck. Damaged sections are replaced before any new material goes down. Because you can't see the deck until the old roof is off, a good estimate should explain how deck repairs are priced if they're needed.
